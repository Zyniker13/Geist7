Research pass: 15 Nov 2026. Status legend: **Verified** (checked against a source this pass), **Secondary** (from a reputable secondary source, not primary), **Unverified** (needs advisor or further checking).

## 1. Art Direction References
- **Karesansui (dry landscape) gardens** of Muromachi-period Kyoto Zen temples: Ryōan-ji (rock garden), Daisen-in (Daitoku-ji), and Tōfuku-ji's Hōjō gardens (Shigemori Mirei, 1939) as modern reference. Use as visual study only; never reproduce a specific temple layout (see §3).
- **Raking patterns (samon):** NAJGA lists seven common patterns: aranamimon (rough waves), ichimatsumon (checkerboard), mizumon (concentric ripples), ryūsui (flowing water), sazanamimon (ripples), seigaihamon (overlapping semicircles), and tachinamimon (standing waves). Use them for rake rendering styles (R-23). Verified.
- **Stone composition:** Sakuteiki vocabulary (omo-ishi, waki-ishi, mae-ishi, sanzon-ishigumi) informs how stones look in groups (odd, asymmetric triads). Verified via NAJGA.
- **Palette:** white granite gravel (shirakawa-suna) `#E9E6DF`, moss `#5F7A3A`, clay-plaster wall `#D8CDB6`, roof tile `#3E4246`, maple `#B5482A`. Keep; matches photographic references.
- **Typography:** light serif display with generous spacing; no faux-Japanese Latin fonts ("chop suey" lettering is on the avoid list).
- **Materials and textures:** granite gravel with groove normal maps, plaster walls with seasonal staining, moss with soft edges, stones photographed and rendered from local (non-temple) quarries.
- **Further study:** Portland Japanese Garden, Brooklyn Botanic Garden Japanese Hill-and-Pond Garden, and the Morikami Museum (Florida) for North American practice.

## 3. Cultural and Historical Context
- **Origins:** dry landscape gardens developed in medieval Japan, strongly associated with Zen temples from the Muromachi period; the Sakuteiki (11th century) is the oldest garden manual. Secondary.
- **Sacred vs secular:** temple gardens are part of religious sites; raking is maintained by temple staff and monks as practice. Residential and modern public karesansui are secular. The game is set in a generic, non-temple courtyard.
- **Living communities:** Japanese garden designers and craftspeople; temples that own famous gardens; North American Japanese garden community (NAJGA).
- **Sensitivities and avoid list:** no monks as characters, no Buddhist iconography or sutra text, no named temples or replicas of famous layouts (Ryōan-ji's 15 stones), no "Zen" as a marketing buzzword, no faux-Japanese typography, no stone lanterns or basins presented as decor without context.
