# 12 Girih — Art, Design, and Culture Research

Research pass: 15 Nov 2026. Sourcing tier FULL. Status legend: Verified, Secondary, Unverified.

## 1. Art Direction References
- **Monuments (study only, no reproduction):** Darb-i Imam shrine, Isfahan (1453); Gunbad-i Kabud, Maragha (1197); Friday Mosque of Isfahan; Alhambra (Granada) for Western Islamic strapwork; Topkapı Scroll (Topkapı Palace Museum library) for Timurid-era design sheets.
- **Collections:** Victoria and Albert Museum (Islamic Middle East gallery; tilework and woodwork); Metropolitan Museum Islamic galleries; Museum of Islamic Art, Doha.
- **Crafts:** gereh-sazi (girih woodwork); banna'i (glazed brick and plain brick patterning); kashi tilework.
- **Palette:** turquoise `#2E9C9C`, lapis `#1F3F7A`, white glaze, ochre brick `#B98653`, manganese purple, gold.
- **Typography:** a clean sans with Persian/Arabic script support (e.g., Vazirmatn) for glossary display; no calligraphic flourishes.
- **Materials:** glazed tile, unglazed brick, inlaid wood (khatam), plaster.

## 2. Design Lineage
- **Girih tiles:** five tiles with equal edges and decorative lines meeting edges at 54°; Lu and Steinhardt (2007) argued girih tiles were used as a design method and that the Darb-i Imam shows near-quasicrystalline self-similar patterns. Verified (Science 315:1106). The interpretation is disputed (e.g., analyses in the Nexus Network Journal, 2018). Verified that dispute exists.
- **Tile-laying games:** Carcassonne (edge-matched placement), Azul (pattern tiles), Penrose-tile puzzles.
- **Design note:** equal edges make every placement edge-legal; difficulty comes from vertices and figure claims.

## 3. Cultural and Historical Context
- Geometric ornament flourished across Islamic architecture from the 10th century; it appears in mosques, shrines, madrasas, palaces, and secular objects. Secondary (Broug 2013; Bonner 2017).
- Sacred vs secular: patterns are not themselves sacred, but they frame sacred spaces and texts. Avoid Qur'anic verses, names of God, mosque or shrine replicas, and mihrab shapes.
- Living communities: master craftspeople in Iran, Morocco (zellige), Uzbekistan, and Türkiye; schools such as The King's Foundation School of Traditional Arts (London).
- Avoid: "Arabesque" as a catch-all, orientalist "Arabian Nights" framing, and conflating Persian, Arab, and Turkish traditions.
